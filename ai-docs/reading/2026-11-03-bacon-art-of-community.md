# Reading: The Art of Community (Jono Bacon, 2nd edition, 2012)

O'Reilly Media, second edition, 9 May 2012 (ISBN 9781449312053). Commercial copyright, all rights reserved; not an open licence as far as the file shows. About 244,000 words, 15 chapters. Read 2026-11-03 with readwright from a copy of Mark's download (`D:/RawStorage/BigDownloads/Unorganized/The-Art-of-Community-Building-the-New-Age-of-Participation-Second-edition/`, epub). Read selectively: the full table of contents, the openings of chs. 1-2, and the sections on social media overkill (ch. 6), honesty and announcing (ch. 7), statistics (ch. 8), tracking health (ch. 9), governance (ch. 10) and conflict and burnout (ch. 11). Events (ch. 12), hiring (ch. 13), the case book (ch. 14) and the revenue sections of ch. 2 were not read. Paraphrased below; nothing is quoted at length.

**Verdict:** long, chatty and written for open-source and corporate community managers, but the parts on trust, honesty, conflict and burnout hold up and transfer well to a solo developer's Discord or subreddit. The tool chapters are dated (Twitter, Facebook and Google+ walkthroughs, mailing lists, forum post counts; Discord does not appear). It agrees with the ethics line and adds a usable conflict procedure. Status in the beat's literature: `distilled`.

## Kept

- **Belonging is the goal; trust is the currency** (ch. 1, "Building Belonging", "Trust Is Everything"). Members stay because they feel part of something, and trust takes months to build and one misstep to lose. For an indie server: the game is the reason people arrive, being heard is the reason they stay. Never spend trust on a growth trick.
- **Honest buzz: no inflated story** (ch. 7, "Honesty"). Do not claim achievements you have not made or more people than you have; in an open community the exaggeration comes out and costs faith. For a game: no "thousands of players" in a server of forty, no implied features, no padded wishlist talk. This is the plugin's ethics line in the book's own words.
- **Relevance over reach; never unsolicited** (ch. 7, "Getting It Right by Not Getting It Wrong"). Target the people who would care and do not message people who did not ask. For an indie: no DM blasts to other servers' members, no invite spam, post only where the place's rules allow it.
- **Do not build yourself up by tearing others down** (ch. 7, "Honesty"). Running down another project makes your community look petty, and members follow the leader's tone. For an indie: no digs at rival games, and ask the same of moderators and regulars who speak for the game.
- **Posting too much switches people off** (ch. 6, "Avoiding social media overkill"). Bacon's own band project posted several times a day to every linked network at once and fans joked about the "propaganda machine". For a devlog or announcements channel: a steady, modest cadence, written for each place, beats volume.
- **Announce in four steps: prepare, build up, announce, review** (ch. 7, "The Buzz Cycle", "Announcing Your Community"). Have the landing place and how-to-join ready first, tease it, announce everywhere that makes sense on one day, then check where it spread and whether the right people came. For an indie: Discord set up with rules and channels before the Steam page links to it; review join sources and first questions afterwards.
- **Quantity is not quality** (ch. 8, "The risks of interpretation"). Post counts and rank labels pump members up but say little about value. For an indie: member count and message volume are not health; look at who answers newcomers, bug reports with detail, people who come back after a patch.
- **Measure what graphs miss: people will not tell you everything** (ch. 9, "Tracking Health"). However open you are, members hold back concerns from the leader, so keep a few trusted regulars who will tell you how the room feels, and ask directly now and then. For a solo dev: one or two moderators who report mood, plus an occasional anonymous survey.
- **A clear conflict procedure, and visible fairness** (ch. 11, "The Conflict Resolution Process", "Lack of Justice"). Calm and reassure, get facts from both sides, discuss, write the agreement down plainly, then check back in a few weeks. Members lose faith fastest when a conflict looks unhandled, so give a route to appeal a moderator's call (for a small server: the developer reviews any mod decision on request). Answer bad-faith campaigns with calm evidence, not heat (ch. 11, "Poisonous people").
- **Watch for burnout, yours and your helpers'** (ch. 11, "Dealing with Burnout", "Work/Life Balance"). It shows as slow changes in behaviour; the answer is noticing and offering a hand, plus real time off. For a solo dev who is also the community manager: set hours for the server, let moderators step back without guilt, and say publicly when you are away.

## Rejected or flagged

- **No conflict with the ethics line found** in the parts read: the book argues against spam, exaggeration and disparaging rivals. The `grep` for sock puppets, astroturfing and fake accounts found nothing either way, so the book neither endorses nor addresses them directly; the plugin's rule stands as is.
- **"Poisonous people" label** (ch. 11): useful for organised bad faith, but the book itself says that voicing concern does not make someone poisonous. Flag: never use the label on critics of the game or of a decision; criticism in the open channel is feedback, not a threat.
- **Meritocracy** (ch. 2): presented as a good default for contributor communities. The term has since been widely criticised for hiding who gets access, and a player community is mostly read-mostly anyway (ch. 2, "Read Versus Write"). Not taken as a model.
- **Councils, elections and subcouncils** (ch. 10, the Ubuntu example): far too heavy for a solo or small studio. Kept only the idea that the rules and the route to appeal are written down where members can see them.
- **Dated tools** (ch. 6 and elsewhere): Twitter, Facebook and Google+ how-tos, linked cross-posting, MySpace, identi.ca, mailing lists, IRC, forum rank labels. The book predates Discord's dominance and today's Reddit and Steam moderation tools. None of the platform specifics enter the kb.
- **Revenue sections** (ch. 2: advertising, selling, donations, sponsorship): not read. If used later, check them against the disclosure rule (sponsorship and paid coverage must be disclosed).
- **Corporate frame**: Bacon wrote as Ubuntu's community manager at Canonical, a company that funded the community it led. Read the advice on "the company investing in the community" with that in mind; a solo developer is both the company and the leader, which makes honesty about commercial motives more important, not less.
