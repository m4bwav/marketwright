# Reading: The Art of Community: Seven Principles for Belonging (Charles Vogl, 2016)

Berrett-Koehler, 2016. About 48,000 words. A preface and introduction lead into Part One (what a community is) and Part Two, which sets out seven principles: boundary, initiation, rituals, temple, stories, symbols and inner rings. Part Three covers avoiding a cult and running communities face to face and online; leader worksheets and a dinner-series case study close the book. Read 2026-11-03 with readwright from a copy of Mark's download (`D:/RawStorage/BigDownloads/Unorganized/Art of Community_ Seven Principles for Belonging, The - Charles Vogl.epub`). Each chapter was read from its start, plus parts of the boundary, inner-rings and online chapters. The appendices and the rest of the long chapters were not read. Paraphrased below; nothing is quoted at length.

**Verdict:** useful, and the best source the beat has on community structure. The seven principles map well onto a game's Discord: who belongs, how newcomers are welcomed, what recurs, where people gather, what stories members share, and how members grow into roles. The book draws on religious traditions and says so openly, and it gives a whole chapter to not building a cult. It has no data on online communities. The introduction is framed by a talk with a Twitch cofounder, but the online material itself is thin. Status in the beat's literature: `distilled`.

## Kept

- **A community is people who care about each other's welfare, not a shared interest alone** (ch. 1 "Understanding Community"). Fans of a game who never talk to each other are an audience, not a community. For a dev, the Discord works when players help and know each other. Count conversations between players, not joins.
- **Name the shared value, and use the boundary to keep insiders safe** (ch. 2 "The Boundary Principle"). A community open to everyone is no community. There is always some shared value, and naming it lets the right people see that they belong. A boundary is there to make the inside safe more than to keep people out, and outsiders who share the value need a clear way in. For a game, write a short statement of what the server is for and how members treat each other, and give visitors a visible way to read before they post.
- **Mark the moment someone joins, and keep it simple** (ch. 3 "The Initiation Principle"). An initiation need not be elaborate. A personal welcome is enough if everyone understands it to mean "you are one of us now". For a small dev, greet new members by name yourself while the server is small, and give a role after a first real contribution, such as a bug report or a first showcase post.
- **Rituals are recurring acts that carry meaning** (ch. 4 "The Rituals Principle"). Rituals tie the present to the community's past and future, and they can be formal or informal. For a game: a weekly devlog post discussed together, a monthly playtest night, a screenshot day, a thank-you post at each milestone. A few regular rituals beat many one-offs.
- **A temple is a place set aside where members know they will find each other** (ch. 5 "The Temple Principle"). Any space becomes one when members gather there and keep the rituals. For a game, keep one home (a server or forum) with channels set aside for specific rituals, rather than a thin presence spread across many platforms.
- **Tell one true origin story, and let members tell theirs** (ch. 6 "The Stories Principle"). Every community needs a story of how it began: what the founder learned, what they did, and how they invited others in. Stories teach a community's values better than rules do. For a game, tell why it exists the same way everywhere, and keep a place where players' own stories (a first win, a funny bug) are saved and retold.
- **Inner rings are paths to serve, not ranks** (ch. 8 "The Inner Rings Principle"). Healthy communities have a progression, from visitor through novice, member and senior member to elder. Each ring brings new ways to contribute and new access, not status for its own sake. Many rings in a small group are silly, and endless striving for the next ring is a trap. For a small dev, two or three rings are enough: playtester, community helper or moderator, and perhaps early builds or a say on a feature. Each is earned by contribution and never sold.
- **Choose leaders by the kind of success they want** (ch. 10 "Managing Community Face-to-Face and Online"). Vogl separates people who want to beat others, people who want the most for themselves, and people who want the whole group to do well. Leaders of the first kind make the community no fun. The chapter also introduces Elinor Ostrom's work on managing shared resources (not read in full here). For a dev, pick moderators who help others, not the most active or loudest players.

## Rejected or flagged

- **Exclusivity as a sales lever.** Boundaries, inner rings and symbols are easy to turn into scarcity and status marketing: paid "inner circle" roles, fake waitlists, badges for money, insider fear of missing out. Vogl frames them around safety and service, not selling, but the tools are strong. For the plugin, roles and rings are earned by contribution and never bought. Paid supporter tiers are fine only when they are labelled as paid and buy thanks, not standing in the community.
- **Initiation that costs too much.** The book's examples include monastic novice periods that last years. In a game community, hazing, grind or a purchase gate before someone counts as a member is out. Welcomes stay light and free.
- **Origin stories that are emotionally rather than factually true** (ch. 6). He allows an origin story to count as true if its emotional or ideological truth holds, while saying it is strongest when the facts are true as well. In marketing that is a loophole. For the plugin, a game's origin story must be factually true: no invented hardship and no made-up founding moment.
- **Rituals and symbols as engagement mechanics.** Daily check-ins, streaks and must-attend events can turn into pressure. In the plugin's sense a ritual is an invitation: skipping it carries no penalty and triggers no guilt message.
- **Cult risk** (ch. 9 "Distinguishing Religion and Avoiding Cult"). The chapter exists because cults use these same principles, and that is the strongest flag in the book. Signs to watch for in a game community: a dev whose word cannot be questioned, members discouraged from other games or communities, criticism treated as betrayal, leaving made hard. None of these is acceptable, and the plugin should say so wherever it hands out these tools.
- **Scale of the examples** (introduction). The book opens with Twitch's cofounder asking how to turn millions of visitors into a community. Advice for a company that size does not transfer directly to a small dev's server of a few hundred.
