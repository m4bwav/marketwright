# Reading: Overdeliver (Brian Kurtz, 2019)

Hay House, 2019. Full title "Overdeliver: Build a Business for a Lifetime Playing the Long Game in Direct Response Marketing", foreword plus an introduction and ten chapters, about 82,000 words. Read 2026-11-03 from a sandboxed copy of Mark's download (`D:/RawStorage/BigDownloads/Unorganized/Overdeliver.epub`): every chapter's headings and key-takeaways recap in full, and the sections named below read in full. The body of the longer chapters (lists, offers, copy) was not read line by line. Paraphrased below; nothing is quoted at length.

**Verdict:** moderate yield, mostly as reinforcement. It is a memoir of thirty years of newsletter direct mail at Boardroom, and most of its machinery (list rental, RFM, regression models, statistical tests, the "bogey" a mailer may lose on a first order) needs volumes a solo indie never has. What carries over is the stance: the people on your list are the asset, keep every promise and then some, the product sells the second purchase, and say no to deals that do not fit your audience even when you need the money. That agrees with the ethics line, and a few concrete habits are worth adding. It has no game data and no sources beyond anecdote. Status in the beat's literature: `distilled`.

## Kept

- **The list is people, and it is the most important asset you own** (ch. 4 "List Building & RFM", ch. 10 close). Treat the names as you would family. His 40/40/20 rule of thumb (list, offer, creative) says who you reach matters more than how clever the copy is. For a game: the mailing list, Discord and wishlisters who opted in are the asset; a small list of real players beats a big list of strangers. Goes in references/plan.md, step 2 "Who and where" and the "Paid, earned, owned" bullet: grow the owned list first.
- **Response lists beat compiled lists** (ch. 4). People who already answered a similar offer respond far better than people chosen by demographics. For a game: players of the three closest games, and people who signed up for the playtest or demo, are the response list; broad "gamers" targeting is the compiled one. Supports plan.md step 2 as written; no new text needed beyond the edit above.
- **Act as if every send costs postage** (ch. 3 "How Paying Postage Made Me a Better Marketer"). When each message is cheap, people get sloppy and annoy the list. Pretending each one is expensive makes you send only what is ready and worth the reader's time, and check its small details and logic first. Goes in references/reach.md, "Devlogs and the post calendar", step 1: before each post or mail-out, ask whether it would be worth sending if it cost money.
- **Ask "what is missing?" before writing copy** (ch. 6 "Creative & Copy"; ch. 3 best practice 3). His copywriter mentor began by asking what the product lacked, and the best headline came out of interviewing the founder, not from the writer. For a game: interview the developer about what is new and hard about the game, and the hook comes from the answers. Goes in references/plan.md, step 1 "The game in four lines".
- **How you promote decides who you get** (ch. 5 "Offers", the two-package story). A gloomy mailing lost overall but won with one slice of the list, so they kept both, each for its own audience. A pitch draws people who match it, and their later loyalty follows. For a game: pitch the real tone of the game, because players drawn by a mismatched pitch refund and review badly. Reinforces the "Building hype" grey zone in kb/ethics.md; no edit needed.
- **Fulfilment is marketing after the marketing; keeping a player is cheaper than finding one** (ch. 8 "Customer Service & Fulfillment"). Answer people, fix what they report, and give more than was promised. He also uses secret shoppers: someone outside walks through the customer's path and reports every snag. For a game: patch notes, replies on the Steam forum and Discord, and having a stranger try the store page, the buy flow and the first ten minutes. Goes in references/reach.md, "The game's own community", as one line on support as marketing; the outside walk-through fits the store review (references/store.md) if the caller wants it.
- **The product sells the renewal** (ch. 9 "Continuity & LTV"): marketers sell the first subscription, editors sell the renewals. For a game: updates and the next game are sold by how good this one is, not by the launch push. Reinforces plan.md "Past launch week".
- **Say no to incongruent deals, even when cash is short** (ch. 9, "The Moose on the Table"). He let a mailer he distrusted use his list for a large one-off fee; a partner's subscriber traced it back and it cost him trust. Lesson: never decide when need for money outranks your principles, and assume everything comes to light. For a game: refuse paid placements, sponsors, bundles or key resellers that the players would see as a betrayal. Goes in kb/ethics.md, "The question" section, as a line backing the embarrassment test.
- **More than one channel; the biggest is not the best** (ch. 7 "Multichannel Marketing"). One channel means one algorithm or rule change can wipe you out, and a smaller audience that stays and buys again beats a large one that does not. Agrees with reach.md "Stock and flow" (a page the user owns); no edit needed.
- **Help others first; competitors coexist** (ch. 1 "Overdeliver", ch. 10 "Playing the Long Game"). Give without keeping score, treat rivals as neighbours, keep in touch with people without wanting something now. Matches the be-a-member-first rule in reach.md "Communities"; no edit.

## Rejected or flagged

- **The "paranoid package"** (ch. 5): a fear headline ("world crisis") sold well to one segment. The segmentation lesson is kept above, but fear-led copy is manipulative and is not taken. A game pitch shows what the game is, not a threat.
- **Barrier to switch** (ch. 8): he means making leaving hard through service so good nobody wants to leave, and says outright it is not captivity. The honest reading (a better game, kept up) is fine. Any version that locks players in (lost progress, hostage cosmetics, nagging to stay, cancellation friction) is a dark pattern under the ethics line. Flagged so it is never read the second way.
- **List rental and buying lists** (ch. 4, ch. 9): renting or buying names is normal in his trade. For a game it means buying email lists or follower lists, which is spam law territory and breaks the no-bought-audience line. Not taken; only opted-in lists.
- **Swipe files and "use what's already working"** (ch. 2): studying winning promotions is fine as study. Copying another game's trailer beats, page copy or capsule look is not. Taken only as "learn the craft", never as copying.
- **Premiums and bonuses** (ch. 5): extra gifts to lift an offer. Real extras (a soundtrack, an art book) are fine when they exist and are delivered. Not as bait with conditions, and never tied to a review or a wishlist count.
- **Outworking everyone** (ch. 1): his first pillar. As with the sleep advice in Kleon, not taken for a solo developer; the plugin keeps a fixed, small marketing budget of time.
- **Spend more up front because lifetime value pays it back** (ch. 9, the "bogey"): needs measured repeat-purchase data a first game does not have. Not taken; it would push a solo dev into paid ads it cannot afford.

Nothing in the book argues for fake scarcity, countdowns or fake reviews; it does not discuss them.
