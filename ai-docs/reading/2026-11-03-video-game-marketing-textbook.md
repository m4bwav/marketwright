# Reading: Video Game Marketing: A Student Textbook (Zackariasson and Dymek, 2017)

Routledge, first edition, 2017 (ISBN 9781138812277 paperback, 9781315748900 ebook), by Peter Zackariasson and Mikolaj Dymek. Commercial copyright, all rights reserved; not an open licence. About 66,000 words, 164 PDF pages, 12 chapters in three parts (Tools, Challenge, Explore). Read 2026-11-03 with readwright from a copy of Mark's download (`D:/RawStorage/BigDownloads/Unorganized/Video Game Marketing A Student Textbook.pdf`). Read selectively: the contents, the opening of the introduction, the opening of ch. 5 (marketing communications) through the paid/earned/owned split and the four communication models, the media relations passage of ch. 5, the consumer-expectations figure and text in ch. 3, the opening of the research types in ch. 6, the opening of ch. 12 (alternate reality games), and `grep` passes for indie, crowdfunding, Steam, early access, reviews, complaints, innovators, word of mouth and manipulation. Chapters 7-9 (postmodern marketing, marketing as practice, the future), ch. 10 (advergames), most of ch. 11 (gamification) and the rest of ch. 4 (brands) were not read. Paraphrased below; nothing is quoted at length.

**Verdict:** an academic marketing-theory primer (4Ps, Maslow, adoption curve, BCG matrix, brand architecture, PR models) dressed in game examples, written for students heading into publishers and studios. Little of it is actionable for a solo developer in 2026 and none of it is about Steam: crowdfunding, Kickstarter, early access, wishlists, festivals and creators do not appear. Two ideas transfer well (plan the audience before the build; talk with players, not at them) and one framework is a useful checklist (paid, earned, owned). One claim, that raising expectations always helps a game's evaluation, conflicts with the ethics line and is rejected. Status in the beat's literature: `skimmed, low yield`.

## Kept

- **Marketing starts before production, not at the end** (Introduction; ch. 1, opening; ch. 5, introduction). The authors argue that a game marketed only once it is nearly finished has a high risk of failing, because you cannot say anything well without knowing whom you are talking to and what about. For an indie: name the audience and the hook in the first prototype month, and test that hook (a GIF, a capsule mock-up, a one-line pitch) before committing the year. Already the spirit of references/plan.md; the book gives it a cleaner "why".
- **Paid, earned, owned** (ch. 5, "Bought/paid, earned/non-paid and owned media communications"). Paid is advertising, earned is attention others give you (press, creators, word of mouth), owned is channels you control (store page, newsletter, Discord, devlog, a game's own menus). The book closes with three questions a developer should answer: budget for paid, a plan to earn attention, and which owned spaces already exist. For an indie: a quick audit line in the plan file; most solo budgets are near zero paid, so the work is earned plus owned, and the owned list (mailing list, Discord, Steam page, a previous game) is usually longer than the developer thinks.
- **Two-way, symmetrical communication over broadcast** (ch. 5, "Four strategic communication models"; continues on the following pages). Of the four PR models (publicity stunt, public information, asymmetrical two-way, symmetrical two-way), the authors favour the one where the audience's view can change the company, not only its messaging; they describe the stunt model as propaganda by another name. For an indie: devlogs and community posts that ask real questions and visibly act on the answers; players should see a patch note that came from their thread. Matches the Bacon note's trust line.
- **The developer is part of the game's culture, not outside it** (ch. 5, the "Consumers" passage). Every post and trailer is a contribution to a player culture, and a developer should be present in player spaces in ongoing dialogue, not only in developer forums or during a crisis. For an indie: be a regular in the two or three communities where the game fits before posting the game there; matches reach.md's community rules.
- **Most talk about your game is not yours to control** (ch. 5, the "Consumers" passage; ch. 1, information sources in the buying process). Reviews, ratings and social talk sit beside the sources you do control (store page, site, posts). For an indie: put the effort into the parts you control being accurate and complete, and earn the rest by the game and by conduct; never try to manufacture the uncontrolled part (which is where the ethics line's review and sock-puppet bans come in).
- **A complaint is a contact, not a burden** (ch. 3, "Pure goods" and the service-focus passage). A service view does not mean the customer is always right, but each complaint is a chance to fix the experience, and an unhappy player tells friends. For an indie: answer negative Steam reviews and bug reports calmly and specifically, ship the fix, say so in the patch notes. Never ask for a review to be changed in exchange for anything.
- **Three research moments: pre-production, production, post-mortem** (ch. 6, research types). Ask about audience and demand before the expensive build, test during it, and write down what worked after release. Secondary sources listed include industry reports, competitors' own marketing and social media discussion in the relevant subgroups. For an indie: look at comparable games' store pages, reviews and community threads before starting; run playtests during; write a short marketing post-mortem with the real numbers after launch.
- **Innovators want to see the game being made** (ch. 2, product life cycle "development" phase; ch. 1, adoption curve). Early adopters care about how a game is built and how to get early access; the early majority waits for reviews by other players. For an indie: devlogs and playtests serve the first group, a polished launch with honest reviews serves the second; do not expect the devlog audience to be the whole market.

## Rejected or flagged

- **"Generating high expectations always seems beneficial"** (ch. 3, consumer expectations, after Figure 3.2). Conflicts with the ethics line. The model it rests on is about evaluation shifting with prior expectation, and the book's own conclusion ignores the gap case where the game falls short of what was promised. For an indie the honest rule is the reverse: set accurate expectations (trailers show only what the game does, no implied features), because the gap between promise and game is what turns into negative reviews and refund requests.
- **Publicity-stunt model** (ch. 5, the press agentry example). The book names it as manipulation and propaganda and notes it still thrives; useful as a warning, never as a template. Fake controversy, staged leaks and invented scarcity fall under the ethics line's fake scarcity and misleading-trailer bans.
- **Media relations as an attention economy** (ch. 5, media relations passage). The book describes press relations as often orchestrated and ethically questioned; it does not address disclosure of paid or gifted coverage. The plugin's disclosure rule stands; nothing here weakens it.
- **AAA and publisher frame**: brand architecture, branded houses, the BCG matrix, advertising-agency structure, retail placement and mission statements (chs. 2 and 4) assume a portfolio and a marketing department. The book itself notes that the advertising budget is a high entry barrier (ch. 5). Not taken into the kb.
- **Dated**: no Steam features, no wishlists, festivals, creators, Discord, TikTok, crowdfunding or early access (the `grep` found one passing mention of Steam). Examples lean on Xbox Live, Facebook and 2010-era cases. None of the platform specifics enter the kb.
- **Advergames, gamification and ARGs** (chs. 10-12): mostly about brands using games to sell other products. An ARG for one's own game is possible but costly for a solo developer; if used, it must not pose as real people or real organisations, and paid actors must not pose as fans (ethics line: sock puppets). Not read in full.
- **No invented numbers**: the book gives no sourced indie sales or conversion figures worth taking into kb/data.md.
