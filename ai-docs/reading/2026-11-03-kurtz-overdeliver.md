# Reading: Overdeliver (Brian Kurtz, 2019)

Brian Kurtz, *Overdeliver: Build a Business for a Lifetime Playing the Long Game in Direct Response Marketing*, foreword by Jay Abraham. Commercial copyright, all rights reserved. EPUB, about 94,300 words: an introduction and ten chapters, ch. 10 split into short essays. Read 2026-11-03 with readwright from a copy of Mark's download (`D:/RawStorage/BigDownloads/Unorganized/Overdeliver.epub`). Read selectively: the table of contents, the closing "Key takeaways" of chs. 1, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 and 9, the book's final takeaways (ch. 10), the Q-test and premium passages of ch. 5 and the "paranoid package" test (ch. 5), plus greps for scarcity, urgency, deadlines, guarantees, premiums, negative option and testing. Chs. 2 (the classic copywriters) and most chapter bodies were not read beyond grep hits. Paraphrased below; nothing is quoted beyond a short phrase.

**Verdict:** low yield. A memoir and manual of a newsletter and book publisher's direct mail business (Boardroom, Bottom Line) for info-product sellers. Its tone is better than the genre's: the list is real people to treat like family, overdeliver on what you promise, play fair for the long run. Grep found no fake-scarcity or countdown advice; "scarcity" appears only as a mind-set to avoid. Most of the method (buying rented lists, mailing waves, bogeys, continuity subscriptions, premiums stacked by the dozen) does not fit a solo developer selling one game on Steam. Three ideas transfer: test the message against a control, treat your own mailing list or Discord as the asset, and segment by how recently and how much people engaged. Status in the beat's literature: `skimmed`.

## Kept

- **Your list is the asset, and it is people** (ch. 4 takeaways; ch. 10 final takeaways). The people who chose to hear from you are worth more than any rented reach; respect them and do not annoy them (ch. 3 takeaways). For an indie: a newsletter, Discord or Steam followers who opted in are worth more than a viral spike; never add people who did not sign up.
- **Response beats demographics** (ch. 4, compiled versus response lists). People who already acted (played the demo, wishlisted, backed) are the best audience for the next ask. For an indie: tell demo players and playtesters first about launch, not a cold audience.
- **Recency and depth of engagement (RFM) works on tiny lists** (ch. 4 takeaways). Kurtz says the method scales down. For an indie: send the beta-key or playtest invite first to people who recently replied or played, not to the whole list.
- **Test against a control, and only test what could move the number** (ch. 1, "the control is your enemy"; ch. 5 takeaways on avoiding tiny tests). Keep the current best version and try to beat it with a different headline or offer, not a button colour. For an indie: test capsule art or the short description in big swings (one variant at a time, a week each, on wishlist conversion), not micro-tweaks with too little traffic to read.
- **Ask the audience before you build the offer** (ch. 5, the Q test: survey your best customers with several short blurbs and a two-choice answer). For an indie: put two or three one-line pitches to playtesters or the Discord before choosing the store page's hook; the honest cousin of fake-door tests, since nobody is charged or misled.
- **Segments may want different messages** (ch. 5, "paranoid package"). An upbeat and a fear-led mailing each won with a different part of the list. Keep the idea, drop the fear: a cosy-game crowd and a roguelike crowd may answer to different screenshots or posts.
- **Do not depend on one channel** (ch. 7 takeaways, a rule Kurtz credits to Dan Kennedy). An algorithm change can wipe out a one-channel business. For an indie: Steam plus a list you own plus one or two communities, not all hope on one subreddit or TikTok.
- **Service after the sale is marketing** (ch. 8 takeaways). Answer players, fix their problems, time messages so they do not land in predictable distractions. Matches the plugin's community section.
- **Relationships over networking** (ch. 10, "Relationship capital"). Help other developers first, treat competitors as neighbours, remember that someone is always watching. Fits the creator and peer outreach advice.

## Rejected or flagged

- **"Create a barrier to switch"** (ch. 8 takeaways): Kurtz means great service, but the phrase invites lock-in. Rejected as worded: no tricks that make leaving, refunding or unsubscribing hard (a dark pattern under the ethics line). Keep only "give players reasons to stay".
- **Fear-led copy** (ch. 5, the "World Crisis" package): reported as a test winner with one segment. Rejected for game marketing: alarm or manufactured anxiety in copy is manipulative urgency.
- **Stacked premiums and bonuses** (ch. 5, "How to use premiums", going from one bonus report to a hundred to inflate perceived value): mostly harmless for a book, but as a pattern it pushes inflated "value" claims. Flag: bonus content in a deluxe edition is fine when real and described exactly; never list padded or trivial items to make a bundle look bigger.
- **"Bill me later" free trials and continuity subscriptions** (ch. 5, free trial issues; ch. 9, renewals and LTV): a negative-option model. Not for a one-off game; any recurring charge must be opt-in and easy to cancel.
- **The bogey: plan to lose money on acquisition** (chs. 3 and 9): sound for a publisher with repeat buyers and measured lifetime value; dangerous for a solo developer with one game and no follow-up product. Not taken; paid ads stay the plugin's anecdote-only section.
- **Buying and renting lists** (ch. 4): in 2026 email it breaks consent rules (GDPR, CAN-SPAM practice) and spam norms. Rejected.
- **Hire world-class copywriters** (ch. 6): advice for a funded business; a solo developer writes their own copy. Not taken.
- **Dated media**: direct mail postage, mailing waves, infomercials, 1980s-2010s response rates. None enter the kb.
