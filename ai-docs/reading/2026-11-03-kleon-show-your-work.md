# Reading: Show Your Work! (Austin Kleon, 2014)

Workman Publishing, 2014. About 17,000 words: ten short chapters plus an opener and "What Now?". Read 2026-11-03 with readwright from a copy of Mark's download (`D:/RawStorage/BigDownloads/Unorganized/show_your_work_10_ways_to_share_your_creativity_an.epub`). Each chapter was read from its start (400 to 900 words), so the later parts of the longer chapters were not read in full. Paraphrased below; nothing is quoted at length.

**Verdict:** short, sound and close to the plugin's own stance. It is a book about habits, not tactics: share the process in small daily pieces, be a good member of your scene before you ask it for anything, and ask for money plainly once the work deserves it. It has no data, and the platform names date from 2014, but the principles have aged well and map almost one to one onto a solo developer's devlog. Nothing in it weakens the ethics line, though two passages need a caveat before they are used for sales. Status in the beat's literature: `distilled`.

## Kept

- **Share the process and teach what you learn, not just the product** (ch. 2 "Think Process, Not Product"; ch. 6 "Teach What You Know"). Work in progress, tools, sketches and influences build a bond that a finished trailer cannot. Giving away technique does not create rivals. People trust the person who teaches them, and they remember that person. For a game, the devlog shows the making: a mechanic prototyped, a level rebuilt, a shader breakdown, a postmortem.
- **One small piece a day, chosen from what you documented, matched to the stage** (ch. 3 "Share Something Small Everyday"). Share influences early, methods and work in progress mid-project, and cut material and lessons after release. Once the game is out, share how players use it. That gives the devlog calendar a ready rule keyed to the game's phase.
- **Answer "what are you working on?", and cap the sharing** (ch. 3). Show the work, not your lunch. His example is a 30-minute timer, then back to the work. The plugin should set a small, fixed sharing budget beside dev time and never let it grow until it eats the dev time.
- **Not every post has to be good, and reactions show you what you have** (ch. 3). Most of what anyone makes is weak, and you often cannot tell which part is good until others see it. A devlog is an honest early read on what catches people's interest.
- **Tell the true story of the work** (ch. 5 "Tell Good Stories"). How a thing is described changes how much people value it. For a game, tell why it exists and what was hard about making it. The ethics line applies here: the story must be true (see below).
- **Be a fan first: an open node, not human spam** (ch. 7 "Don't Turn Into Human Spam"). Take part in your scene, point to other people's work, listen, and care about the quality of your followers, not their number. Do not follow people only to get something from them. This is the plugin's anti-spam rule in one image, and on the book's own terms it argues against bought followers.
- **Weigh critics by who they are, block trolls, keep shipping** (ch. 8 "Learn to Take a Punch"). Put out a lot of work so criticism stops feeling fatal. Value feedback from people who care about you and the work. Do not feed trolls; deleting abuse is fair. Your work is something you do, not who you are. For a dev, take feedback from people who actually play the game and moderate abuse without guilt.
- **Ask for money plainly, at a fair price, and keep an email list** (ch. 9 "Sell Out"). Tip jars, crowdfunding and plain "buy now" buttons are all fine once there is an audience and the work is worth paying for. Crowdfunding works best after you have gathered people, and backers will reasonably expect a say. Collect email addresses from people who want to hear from you, even before you have anything to sell. For a game, wishlist and mailing-list asks are honest when they are plain and opt-in.

## Rejected or flagged

- **"Push what they hate even further"** (ch. 8): he suggests that when some people hate an element of the work, it can be fun to lean into it. As a creative stance that is fine. As a marketing tactic (rage-bait, picking fights for attention) it fails the "would the community respect it" test. Not taken as a reach tactic.
- **Stories raise perceived value** (ch. 5, the Significant Objects experiment: invented stories raised the resale price of thrift-store objects). The experiment shows that framing can inflate value. For the plugin, use true stories only. Any claimed history, origin, reaction or award must be real and checkable.
- **Turning comments off** (ch. 8): fine for a personal blog, but in a game community it cuts off bug reports and players' voices. Kept as a moderation option, not a default. Players should still have somewhere to talk.
- **Finding time by losing sleep** (ch. 3): his advice to find time in the cracks of the day includes skipping sleep. Not taken for a solo dev. The plugin keeps its small fixed sharing budget instead.
- **Crowdfunding** (ch. 9): the book is upbeat about it and says nothing about stretch goals or pressure tactics, for or against. The plugin's own rules still apply: rewards and dates you can deliver, honest progress reports, no pressure on backers.
- **Platform advice** (ch. 3, e.g. writers on Twitter, visual artists on Tumblr): dated 2014. None of it enters the kb. Choose platforms by where the game's players actually are.
